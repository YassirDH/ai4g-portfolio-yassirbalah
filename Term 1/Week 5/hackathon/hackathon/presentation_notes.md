# Speaker notes

The slides are in `presentation.pdf`. For each slide: what to say, roughly how long, and questions you can expect. All numbers come from the notebook.

## 1. Who struggles to get by?

Opening (about 20 seconds). Briefly introduce yourself and your partner. One sentence: we predict which Dutch residents struggle to get by on their household income, so that a municipality's early-detection team can send a voluntary offer of help before payment arrears appear.

## 2. The signal only comes once the debt exists

Problem and user (about 1 minute). Since 1 January 2021, the Dutch Municipal Debt Assistance Act obliges a municipality to invite residents when a landlord, energy supplier, water company or health insurer reports payment arrears. By then the debt already exists. Our question: can simple facts about a household point the team to people who struggle earlier? Context: Statistics Netherlands (CBS) counted 551,000 people in poverty in 2024 and 1.1 million just above the poverty line; 35% of them say they have trouble making ends meet. Possible question: "Why SDG 8 and not SDG 1 (poverty)?" Answer: we predict whether someone can live on their work, pension or benefits, which is economic security (target 8.5). Poverty is related, but our target is a feeling about income, not a poverty line.

## 3. 1 in 10 struggles, so no accuracy

Data and metric (about 1 minute). The ESS draws a new random sample of people aged 15+ in the Netherlands every two years; we use rounds 4 to 11. 13,890 rows; 150 without an answer to the target question were removed, leaving 13,740. Target: the question about household income; "difficult" or "very difficult" = 1. Cleaning: refusal and don't-know codes (7/8/9, 77/88/99, 999) became missing; working hours 666 = no job = 0 hours; 8 working weeks above 100 hours became missing. Threshold: with the default threshold of 50%, KNN in particular would flag almost nobody, and the comparison would not be fair. The threshold is a fixed value (10%, the average share of struggling people) and was not tuned on the test set. The notebook (step 6) contains a check on the training data: for logistic regression 10% is also the best threshold (F2 0.510; 0.499 at 8%, 0.487 at 15%). Below 10% a third or more of all residents gets a letter; above 10% many more people are missed. Possible question: "Why not class_weight='balanced' or SMOTE?" Answer: a fixed threshold on the predicted chance does the same job (it flags more positives), is the same for all models and is easy to explain. Possible question: "Why F2 and not F1?" F1 weighs recall and precision equally; for us, missing someone is worse.

## 4. Five candidates, same folds, same threshold

Comparison table (about 1.5 minutes). All models: the same 5 folds (StratifiedKFold, random_state 42), the same F2 scorer in GridSearchCV, the same pipeline (median + StandardScaler for numbers, most frequent answer + one-hot encoding for categories) and the same threshold of 10%. The simple rule is the real bar: if machine learning cannot beat it, the municipality should just use the rule. KNN barely beats the rule on the test set (0.46 vs 0.45). Grids: KNN k from 5 to 501 (best 301; from about 50 onward the error bars overlap, so the differences are small); logistic regression C from 0.001 to 100 (flat from 0.1 upward); decision tree max_depth from 2 to 10 and unlimited (best 6; unlimited drops to 0.29 because the tree memorises the training data). Why a decision tree as our own model: it was covered in the lecture, it can learn combinations ("retired and living alone") and it can be drawn and explained as a flow chart. Possible question: "Why is the test score lower than CV?" For KNN and logistic regression it is 0.02 to 0.03 lower; with only 281 positives in the test set that is normal variation. Leakage would give a suspiciously high test score instead. And: "Why is the decision tree higher on the test set than in CV?" Probably luck with this test set: train 0.51, CV 0.47 ± 0.017, test 0.50.

## 5. Logistic regression on the test set

Confusion matrix (about 1 minute). Rows = reality, columns = what the model says. 188 correctly flagged, 93 missed (false negatives, the worst mistake for us), 595 get a letter although they are getting by (false positives), 1,872 correctly left out. The model flags 783 of the 2,748 people, 28%. In municipal terms: of every 4 letters, 1 goes to someone who really struggles. That is only acceptable because the offer is voluntary and cheap. If the team cannot handle that many letters, it can raise the threshold: fewer letters, but more people missed. Possible question: "Isn't 24% precision bad?" Yes, if this were about sanctions. For a voluntary letter, missing someone weighs more, and that is exactly what F2 expresses.

## 6. Our advice: logistic regression

Advice (about 1 minute). We do not pick the model with the highest test score (the decision tree, 0.50 vs 0.49, a difference smaller than the spread over the folds). We pick the model that misses the fewest people, has the best and most stable CV score, does not overfit and can be explained. It also beats the simple rule: 188 instead of 134 struggling people found. Explainability matters: a resident who asks "why did I get this letter?" deserves an answer, and so does a council member. KNN drops out: lower scores, it misses 132 people, it is slow (it compares every new person with all 11,000 training people) and can only be explained as "people like you". Largest weights in the model: unemployment benefit as main income +0.95, lowest education level +0.81, other benefits +0.68, unemployed +0.63, sick or disabled +0.56; studying -1.08, master's degree or higher -1.06, wages as main income -0.83, never unemployed for more than 3 months -0.79, retired -0.78. Possible question: "Why not the decision tree, it scores higher on the test set?" 0.50 vs 0.49 is smaller than the spread (± 0.017); in CV the tree is clearly lower (0.47 vs 0.51) and it misses 115 people instead of 93.

## 8. Who does the model miss?

Errors and fairness (about 1.5 minutes). Who do we miss? Mainly working people and retired people. Of the 93 missed people, 59% have a paid job and 26% are retired; 61% live on wages and 25% on a pension. On paper they look like the large group that is doing fine; their problems (rent, debts, a divorce, health costs, the size of the wage) are not in the data. Note: the test set contains only 33 struggling retired people, so 27% is uncertain. Unemployed and sick people are found almost all (recall 1.00 and 0.98). Country of birth: people born outside NL are flagged more often (43% vs 27%), but they also struggle more often (25% vs 9%) and the model finds them better (recall 0.77 vs 0.64; precision 0.46 vs 0.20). For a voluntary offer, being flagged is not a punishment, but it can feel like being singled out. So: only for help, never for checks. Proxy check: a logistic regression that predicts country of birth from our 8 features reaches ROC-AUC 0.68. Leaving a column out is not the same as removing the information.

## 9. Does the model still work today?

Does it still work today? (about 45 seconds). We had planned this check in step 2: keep essround aside to see whether the model works equally well in every year. In the notebook it is in step 8, with the same group_report function as for sex and country of birth. Result: in 2008 to 2018 the model finds 61 to 79% of the struggling people. In 2020 and 2023 only 46 and 45%, and precision drops to 0.10 and 0.12: about 1 in 9 letters is on target. Why? In those years fewer people struggle (4 and 6%, against 9 to 15% before), and the model learned mostly from years in which more people struggled. Be honest: the groups are small (13 and 20 struggling people in the test set), so the numbers are uncertain. But those years are the closest to the moment a municipality would use the model. Hence our advice: first test it on recent data from the municipality itself. This links to the question from the lecture: what happens when you use a model on a different population than the one it learned from, and whose job is it to check?

## 10. Income, sex and country of birth stay out.

The decision we defend hardest (about 1 minute, take your time here). Income would raise the score a lot: CV F2 from 0.510 to 0.590, measured with cross-validation on the training set; the test set was not touched for this. It is also almost the answer itself: in the lowest income decile half of the people struggle (52%), in the highest about 1%. We still leave it out: the municipality does not have the income decile of every resident, and collecting it only to rank residents goes further than the law, which works with concrete payment-arrears signals. Sex and country of birth: selecting on them is not allowed (equal treatment); the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal showed what happens when nationality is used in a risk model. We do use them separately, to check whether the model is fair (previous slides). Possible follow-up: "Isn't origin still in there through other columns?" Partly, yes: ROC-AUC 0.68. That is why this model may only be used for a voluntary offer, never for checks or sanctions. Possible follow-up: "Doesn't a municipality know who receives benefits?" Yes, for social assistance it does; that is exactly why those features are in the model. Real use would first require a privacy impact assessment (DPIA).

## 11. How to use it, and how not to

Closing (about 30 seconds). Summary in one sentence: logistic regression finds 2 in 3 struggling people without using income, sex or origin, but it is only suitable for a voluntary offer of help next to existing signals. Limitations to have ready: the ESS is a national sample, while the user is one municipality; people in institutions, homeless people, asylum seekers and people who could not do the interview in Dutch are missing; the data ends in 2023, before the full impact of inflation and energy prices, and the model already works worse in 2020 and 2023; the target is a self-reported feeling, not an administrative fact; survey weights were not used. True: there are 2 respondents aged 14 in the data (round 7). The ESS samples people aged 15+, but age is calculated at the interview; on 13,740 rows it makes no difference.
